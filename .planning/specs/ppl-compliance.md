## Problem

Amendment 13 to the Israeli Privacy Protection Law has been in force since **14 August 2025**. The pilot collects a bride's name, mobile number and free-text notes on a public form, keeps them forever, and tells her nothing. Concretely, verified against the code:

- **There is no privacy notice anywhere.** `Frontend/apps/storefront/src/router.tsx:23` declares six routes and none of them is `/privacy`; the footer in `StorefrontLayout.tsx:146-183` links `/about` and `/accessibility` only. Amendment 13 requires notice **at the moment of collection**, and the moment of collection is `POST /storefront/bookings` and `POST /storefront/otp/send` — both anonymous, both live.
- **There is no marketing consent.** `Customer` (`app/models/customer.py`) has three columns: `tenant_id`, `phone`, `name`. Israel's Communications Law §30A requires a separate, unbundled, default-off opt-in before any marketing message, and `security-checklist-v1.md:40` names it. The booking form's only checkbox is the *required* cancellation-policy consent (`BookPage.tsx:1156`), which is exactly the bundling the statute forbids.
- **Nothing is ever deleted.** `grep -rn "delete(" Backend/app/db/repositories/` returns five hits and every one of them is `soft_delete`. `otp_codes` rows outlive their 5-minute codes forever (`OTP_TTL_SECONDS = 300`, `app/notifications/validation.py`), expired `sessions` accumulate, `message_log` holds every SMS body and phone number indefinitely. `architecture.md:40` already commits to "soft delete (`deleted_at`) **with a real PII-scrub path for PPL erasure**" — the scrub path does not exist.
- **There is no data-subject-rights path.** A bride who asks the boutique to show her, correct, or erase her data has no answer, and the owner has no button. §13/§14 give her the right and a statutory clock.

Five rows of `security-checklist-v1.md` (§Data protection lines 39–43) are touched here, and F21 cannot close without them — but **only three of the five go green at F20**. The per-row accounting is below, because "four rows go green" was the first draft's claim and it was wrong twice over: it undercounted (row 43's incident-response procedure ships here too) and it overclaimed (rows 40 and 42 each contain a clause F20 is explicitly not chartered to discharge).

## Goal

Every shipped collection point states, in Hebrew, at the moment it collects: who the controller is, why, whether answering is voluntary, and to whom the data goes — with a full notice on a public `/privacy` page. Both the collection notice and the boutique's data-processing-agreement disclosure ship as **platform-written Hebrew defaults, overridable per boutique** from an owner-only settings surface (Interview Q8). A separate, unbundled, default-off marketing opt-in is captured with a timestamp and a source. A **reusable, per-data-class retention job** runs in the existing worker and enforces pre-decided #10's periods as tunable settings, with two first-class actions — **purge** (the row is the personal data) and **scrub** (the row is a business record containing personal data) — because F38 and F33 both need the machinery and neither should have to invent it. Data-subject access and erasure are owner-invoked, audited actions on the manage console, backed by a written procedure in the repo.

## What already exists to build on (verified against code)

- **The atomic settings merge is already built for this.** `TenantsRepository.merge_settings` (`app/db/repositories/tenants.py:69-95`) is one `UPDATE tenants SET settings = settings || :patch::jsonb` and its docstring names this feature by number: *"a concurrent writer of a sibling top-level key (E4 #17/#20 will add them) can never be clobbered."* It takes `profile=` and `toggles=` keyword arguments; F20 adds `privacy=`. No Python read-modify-write, ever. **Read what it protects precisely**: `||` replaces whole *top-level* keys, so the guarantee is that a concurrent writer of `profile` cannot clobber `privacy` — it says nothing about two fields *inside* `privacy`, and `boutique/router.py:61-64` is the shipped acknowledgement of that (`exclude_unset`, "only fields the client actually sent may enter the patch"). D16 is where F20 handles the half the merge does not.
- **The route-level owner-only tightening precedent is shipped.** `POST /manage/terms` carries `dependencies=[Depends(require_role(StaffRole.OWNER))]` on the route while its router admits `(OWNER, SHIFT_MANAGER)` (`app/boutique/router.py:215` vs `:32-35`); both gates run additively (F31 D4). `test_staff_role_gating.py` walks the live route table and reads `allowed_roles` off each gate, with an `OWNER_ONLY` literal set at line ~54 and an `UNGATED_ALLOWLIST` at line 75.
- **The worker is a real deployed process with one job.** `app/worker.py:90-108` loads `Settings`, calls `ensure_safe_database_role()`, and loops `poll_once` every `worker_poll_interval_seconds`. `poll_once` enumerates `tenants.list_active()` and does per-tenant work inside `tenant_session` because `tenants` is deliberately RLS-free (F16 D6). F20 registers a **second** job in the same loop — it introduces no process.
- **Chunked-batch discipline is shipped.** `app/booking/backfill.py:36-38` sets `CHUNK_SIZE = 500` / `MAX_CHUNKS = 50` with a one-page-per-transaction loop; F20's retention runner copies that shape verbatim.
- **`app_user` can hard-DELETE — except where a migration revoked it.** `0002_tenants_app_role.py:70,80` grants `SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE` plus default privileges. Two tables were deliberately narrowed: `terms_versions` (`0005:126-127` — `REVOKE ALL` then `GRANT SELECT, INSERT`) and `platform_audit_log` (`0004:39-40` — INSERT only, not even SELECT). The retention registry must therefore never name either, and a test asserts it doesn't.
- **`audit_log.action` is plain TEXT with no CHECK** (`0003_auth.py:71-80`), which `constants.py`'s `AuditAction` comment states explicitly — so new actions need no migration. Same for `platform_audit_log.action` (`0004`).
- **The tenant context already carries `settings` on every request.** `RepositoryTenantResolver` builds `TenantContext(id, slug, name, settings)` per request with **no cache** (`app/tenancy/resolver.py:8-10`: "Caching is deliberately deferred to E5"). The public privacy route therefore needs zero database round-trips of its own, and an owner's edit is visible on the next request.
- **A statutory public page already exists as a template.** `AccessibilityPage.tsx` is a flat route, footer-linked, one `<h1>`, `<h2>` per section, real lists, layout-level `useBoutique()` for the responsible party, and a deliberate refusal to render a spinner or an error in place of the statement. `/privacy` is its twin and copies its argument wholesale.
- **Indexes for the retention scans mostly exist**: `idx_message_log_tenant_created` (`0007:53`), `idx_bookings_tenant_starts` (`0008:96`), `idx_bookings_tenant_customer` (`0008:102`), `idx_sessions_expires_at` (`0003:66`).
- **Existing consent evidence**: `bookings.terms_version_accepted` + `terms_accepted_at` (`0008`), pinned against the append-only `terms_versions` table. Half of checklist row 40 ("timestamp + terms-version") is already true for the *policy* consent.

## Where the interview and the code disagree

Recorded rather than silently reconciled, per the interview's own instruction. In both cases the codebase-consistent reading is taken.

1. **"catalog enquiry" is not a collection point, because it does not exist.** The feature brief names four shipped collection points including a catalog enquiry. `grep -rn "enquiry\|inquiry\|contact_form"` over `Backend/app`, `Frontend/apps` and `Frontend/packages` returns nothing, and the storefront read router (`app/storefront/router.py:268-328`) exposes six GETs and no mutation. The catalog's only contact affordances are the `tel:` and `instagram.com/{handle}` links `ContactCard`/`StorefrontLayout` build from `settings.profile` — outbound links that collect nothing. **Three collection points, not four** (D1).
2. **"OTP codes 15 minutes" (pre-decided #10) is a retention clock, not the code TTL.** The shipped code TTL is `OTP_TTL_SECONDS = 300` and the verification token's is `VERIFICATION_TOKEN_TTL_SECONDS = 600` (`app/notifications/validation.py`), both deliberately non-tunable ("`Settings` carries deployment identity, never product policy"). Reading #10 as a five-minute change to `OTP_TTL_SECONDS` would contradict the epic's own "≤ 5 minutes" requirement and checklist row 16. Read instead as: the **row** is purged 15 minutes after `created_at`. F20 changes neither TTL (D7).

   **The margin is zero, not five minutes, and the first draft's arithmetic was wrong.** `service.py:237` mints `expires_at = created_at + 300`; `:298` mints `verification_expires_at = <verify instant> + 600` **at verify time**, and `:292` refuses to verify once `row.expires_at <= now`. So the latest instant a verification token can be alive is strictly less than `created_at + 300 + 600 = created_at + 900` — exactly `retention_otp_seconds`. 15 minutes is therefore *exactly sufficient and not one second more*: safe only because verify refuses at the boundary rather than at the boundary plus epsilon. That is not a number to leave as a free-typing env var (see the floors in D9).

Points 1–3 are one screen flow, so **one notice placement discharges all three**, and that is the whole reason the block goes on `details` (the earliest step at which any personal datum is typed) rather than on `verify` (where the server first receives one).

### Data (migration `0012_privacy_consent.py`)

Raw-SQL in the 0010/0011 house style. **No new table**: the privacy text lives in `tenants.settings` (Interview Q8, pre-decided #19) and everything else is columns on `customers`.

```sql
-- Marketing consent: separate, unbundled, default OFF. Default-off is
-- STRUCTURAL — a NULL timestamp is the absence of consent, so there is no
-- boolean whose server_default could be flipped by a later migration.
ALTER TABLE customers ADD COLUMN marketing_consent_at TIMESTAMPTZ;
ALTER TABLE customers ADD COLUMN marketing_consent_source TEXT;
-- NAMED, and a separate statement, exactly like 0011:23-24. An inline CHECK on
-- ADD COLUMN gets a Postgres-generated name, and F33's "one-line widening" then
-- depends on guessing it — which is the whole reason 0011 named its own.
ALTER TABLE customers ADD CONSTRAINT customers_marketing_consent_source_check
  CHECK (marketing_consent_source IN ('booking_form'));
-- Withdrawal is recorded, never erased: proving consent EXISTED when a message
-- was sent is the Spam-Law evidence, so clearing marketing_consent_at would
-- destroy the defence. Effective consent = at IS NOT NULL AND withdrawn IS NULL.
ALTER TABLE customers ADD COLUMN marketing_consent_withdrawn_at TIMESTAMPTZ;
-- Erasure evidence, and the flag that makes the placeholder name honest.
ALTER TABLE customers ADD COLUMN erased_at TIMESTAMPTZ;

-- Cannot withdraw what was never given.
ALTER TABLE customers ADD CONSTRAINT customers_marketing_withdraw_check
  CHECK (marketing_consent_withdrawn_at IS NULL OR marketing_consent_at IS NOT NULL);
```

Exactly two constraints ship, **both named and both as their own `ADD CONSTRAINT` statement** (the 0011 shape, which also buys the populated-table validation this spec claims for them below). A third — `CHECK (marketing_consent_at IS NULL OR marketing_consent_source IS NOT NULL)`, "a consent must carry a source" — is the obvious next thought and is **declined**: it makes F33's one-line CHECK widening a two-constraint migration, and it pins an invariant the single writer (`CustomersRepository.upsert`, which sets both fields in one statement) cannot violate.

`marketing_consent_source` starts with **one** allowed value. F33's walk-in consent (pre-decided #26) widens the CHECK by migration, exactly as `ScheduledMessageKind` does (F16 D9) and as 0011 did for `StaffRole`. Pre-adding `'walk_in'` now is the un-lazy thing; adding the *column* now rather than in F33 is not, because the alternative is F33 shipping a column plus a data backfill stamping every pre-existing consent `'booking_form'`.

**No new indexes.** The three retention scans that need one already have one (see "What already exists"); `otp_codes` rows live ≤15 minutes so its table never grows past a page, `scheduled_messages` is purged by a booking-id set the same statement already materialised, and the orphan-customer anti-join probes `idx_bookings_tenant_customer`. A marketing-audience index (`WHERE marketing_consent_at IS NOT NULL AND withdrawn IS NULL`) is deliberately **not** created: nothing sends marketing in v1 and an index with no reader is the un-lazy thing. <!-- ponytail: per-tenant seq scan on otp_codes and scheduled_messages. Ceiling: a tenant with tens of thousands of live rows in either; neither is reachable at pilot volume. -->

`ADD CONSTRAINT` validates existing rows, so both CHECKs are proven against a **populated** table in `test_migrations.py`, the 0011 precedent (`test_adding_the_role_check_validates_existing_rows`). Every pre-0012 customer row has three NULLs and satisfies both trivially.

### The privacy text: platform default + per-boutique override (D3, D4, D5)

Two documents, one storage key, one resolver.

```python
# app/privacy/text.py — pure constants + one pure function, no I/O.
PLATFORM_NOTICE_HE: str        # the §11 collection notice, Hebrew, ~1.5 KB
PLATFORM_DPA_HE: str           # the boutique's processor-disclosure prose, Hebrew
PLATFORM_SUBPROCESSORS_HE: str # the sub-processor list — NOT overridable (D14)

@dataclasses.dataclass(frozen=True)
class ResolvedPrivacy:
    notice_text: str
    dpa_text: str
    subprocessors_text: str     # always PLATFORM_SUBPROCESSORS_HE
    notice_is_default: bool
    dpa_is_default: bool

def resolve_privacy(settings: dict[str, Any]) -> ResolvedPrivacy: ...
```

**The sub-processor list is platform-owned and structurally un-overridable (D14).** Risk 7 records the platform's standing duty to amend the list whenever a processor is added (the SMS adapter, F46 Twilio WhatsApp, F47 Cloudflare Stream, F17/F18 Grow) — but an *overridden* `dpa_text` is a frozen copy, so under a single-blob design that amendment reaches only the boutiques that never overrode. The moment a real SMS provider goes live, every overriding boutique's public disclosure becomes factually false, and nothing in CI can detect it (Risk 7 says so itself). So the page renders **two blocks**: the boutique's overridable DPA prose, then a platform-owned sub-processor section beneath it. An amendment then reaches every tenant by construction rather than by remembering. This narrows what Q8's "overridable" covers, deliberately: a boutique may rewrite what it promises about processing, and may not misstate who the processors are. Flagged for the user at Gate 1 (question 3) because Q8 is a legal-surface answer and this reads it narrowly.

`settings["privacy"]` is `{"notice_text"?: str, "dpa_text"?: str}`. **A blank override means "use the platform default", never "publish nothing"** — the same `""`-to-null collapse `profile_text` already applies to profile fields, and here it is load-bearing twice over: `settings || patch` can add and replace a JSONB key but cannot *remove* one, so `""` is the only reachable revert sentinel; and a boutique able to publish an empty privacy notice would produce exactly the non-compliance the page exists to declare against (`AccessibilityPage.tsx:12-14` makes the identical argument about a statement showing «fill this in»).

**Interview Q8 says the DPA is overridable per boutique, which only parses under one reading, and that reading is the architecture's own.** `architecture.md:20` fixes "Boutique = controller, platform = processor (**DPA in ToS**)". The platform's contract *toward* the boutique is not something a boutique can rewrite; what it can and must adapt is the clause in **its own** terms of service disclosing to customers that a processor handles their data, who the sub-processors are, and what security measures apply. That clause is the overridable `dpa_text`, it renders as the second section of the public `/privacy` page, and the platform default names today's real sub-processors only (D5).

**The default is not lawyer-reviewed, and the spec says so where it matters.** The disclaimer is rendered **in the manage app, prominently, above the two textareas** — it tells the owner she is the controller, that the platform's Hebrew is a starting point drafted by the platform and not by counsel, and that she should have it reviewed. It is **not** rendered on the public page: a public privacy notice that announces its own legal unreliability is worse than no notice, and the person who needs the warning is the controller, not the data subject. `LOOP-STATE.md`'s standing `user_actions` entry ("Get counsel to review the F16 SMS bodies and the F20 privacy default before either goes live") is the tracked clearance and is **not** discharged by this feature.

**The notice is stored and rendered as plain text.** Never HTML, never `dangerouslySetInnerHTML`. The page splits on a blank line into `<p>` elements and React escapes the rest, so a `<script>` in an override renders as visible characters. Pinned by a frontend test, because the alternative — rich text in a tenant-controlled field on a public page — is stored XSS with a legal document as the vector, and `validate_maps_url`'s scheme allowlist exists in this codebase for the smaller version of the same problem.

**No versioning, and that is a recorded cost.** `terms_versions` is append-only, DB-enforced, and pinned per booking because it carries **money** consequences a customer can dispute. The privacy notice carries none, Interview Q8 explicitly locates it in settings, and versioning it would mean a table, a `bookings` column, a write inside the booking transaction, and a forced re-notice on every typo fix. Declined; the consequence — the exact notice text shown at a past collection is not reconstructable — is Risk 3, with the upgrade path named.

### API surface

Four new `/manage` routes on the **boutique router** (it is already `tenants.settings`' router and already carries the owner-only-tightening idiom), and **no new storefront route at all** (D13).

```
GET  /manage/privacy                       (OWNER, SHIFT_MANAGER)  — router-level gate, terms-GET precedent
PUT  /manage/privacy                       (OWNER,)                — route-level tightening
POST /manage/privacy/subject-export        (OWNER,)                — route-level tightening
POST /manage/privacy/subject-erase         (OWNER,)                — route-level tightening
POST /manage/privacy/marketing-withdraw    (OWNER, SHIFT_MANAGER)  — NO route-level gate; inherits the router's (Gate 1 Q4)
```

`GET /manage/privacy` → 200
```json
{ "notice_text": "…", "notice_is_default": true,
  "dpa_text": "…",   "dpa_is_default": true }
```

`PUT /manage/privacy` body — `ForbidExtraModel`, **both keys REQUIRED**, `null` and `""` both meaning "revert to the platform default"; response is `GET`'s shape re-resolved from the merged settings:
```json
{ "notice_text": "…", "dpa_text": null }
```

**Both keys are required, and that is a correctness requirement, not a style choice (D16).** `merge_settings` is one `settings = settings || :patch::jsonb`, and `||` replaces **whole top-level keys** — `boutique/router.py:61-64` says so in as many words. A patch of `{"privacy": {"notice_text": X}}` therefore replaces the entire `privacy` object, and `resolve_privacy` reads the now-absent `dpa_text` as "use the platform default". An owner who overrides her DPA clause on Monday and edits only her notice on Tuesday silently loses Monday's clause, and `/privacy` reverts to un-reviewed platform Hebrew with no error anywhere. F7's atomic merge protects *sibling top-level* keys (`profile`, `toggles`) and cannot protect fields *inside* one; the first draft's only merge test asserted the former, so it passed while this was broken.

Requiring both keys makes the PUT a whole-object replacement, which is what `PUT` means and what the shipped manage forms already do — `profile_text`'s docstring records that "the manage form seeds every blank field to `""` before submitting". **Declined**: a nested-merge argument on `merge_settings` (`settings || jsonb_build_object('privacy', COALESCE(settings->'privacy','{}'::jsonb) || :patch)`) — it stays one statement and would also be correct, but it changes a shipped repository method used by another feature to serve one caller, and the required-keys contract is zero new SQL. **The cost is recorded**: any future third field under `privacy` must either also be required on this PUT or force the nested merge. A test pins it — a PUT that omits a key is a 422, and a notice-only override must never be reachable.

**`notice_text` and `dpa_text` ship on `GET /storefront/boutique`; there is no `/storefront/privacy` (D13).** The first draft declined this to spare the catalog's first paint, and that reasoning does not survive two facts. First, the collection notice must render **inside the booking flow** (`details` step), so it is on the hot path whether or not a second endpoint exists. Second, `PrivacyPage` copies `AccessibilityPage`'s deliberate refusal to render a spinner or an error in place of the document — but `AccessibilityPage` has *no network dependency* (its content is i18n keys plus layout-level `useBoutique()`), so a page-level `getPrivacy()` would turn the one argument being copied into the one that does not transfer: a 500, a dropped connection, or a 429 off the shared per-tenant storefront read budget (`storefront/router.py:137`, `Depends(_throttle)`, 6000/60s) would render a legally-required document blank.

`StorefrontLayout` mounts **above** the router in `App.tsx`, so every route — catalog, dress, `/book/*`, `/b/{token}`, `/privacy` — already has `useBoutique()`. Folding the two documents into that one existing fetch means: one source of truth for the notice, no new endpoint, no new failure mode, and `AccessibilityPage`'s no-error-state argument transfers intact because the failure mode is now identical (the layout fetch fails → the whole page fails, not the document alone). The payload cost is bounded by the byte cap, which drops to 8 KB per field for an unrelated reason (see the constants table) — typical is ~1.5 KB of Hebrew, against a catalog page that ships dress imagery.

```json
// GET /storefront/boutique — existing response, two new keys
{ "name": "…", "essence": "…", "description": "…", "phone": "…", "address": "…",
  "privacy_notice_text": "…", "privacy_dpa_text": "…", "privacy_subprocessors_text": "…" }
```

`public_boutique`'s docstring rule holds — these are three more explicitly-read keys out of the JSONB blob, resolved through `resolve_privacy`, never a blind passthrough.

`message_log.provider_message_id` and `message_log.error` are **absent by construction**, not filtered late — the response model has no field for them. They are operator diagnostics about a carrier, not the subject's personal data, and F53's note ("never expose `provider_message_id`/`error`") already fixed this posture. `body` **is** included: it is the message she received.

`POST /manage/privacy/subject-erase` body `{"customer_id": "…", "reason": "…"}` → 200 with a count summary.

**The erase is keyed on `customer_id`, not on the phone, and this is a BLOCKER-grade correction (D17).** Step 2 overwrites `customers.phone` with a placeholder, and `CustomersRepository.by_phone` (`customers.py:14-22`) is exact equality on `phone` — so after the first erase there is **no path from her real number back to her row**. The first draft's stated idempotency ("a second call finds `erased_at` set and returns the same summary with zero counts") was therefore unimplementable: a repeat call by phone finds nothing at all, and a lookup that finds nothing is indistinguishable from a typo. Both available answers were wrong. A 404 contradicts the spec's own contract and drops F16 D1's "she will click it more than once" argument; a 200-with-zero-counts for any unresolvable phone means an owner who mistypes one digit gets a success screen **and step 6 writes an `audit_log` row `action='privacy_subject_erased'` for a subject that was never touched** — a fabricated §14 compliance record, on the one path where the record is the whole point.

Keying on the id — which the scrub does not destroy, and which is what `erased_at` hangs off — makes every outcome honest: an unknown phone 404s at the **export/lookup** step where a 404 is correct and expected; a double-tap carries the same id and is a real read of a real row, so the 200-with-zero-counts is true; and no audit row can be written for a subject that does not exist. The manage UI's flow is unchanged in shape — phone in, subject shown, typed confirmation, erase — because the lookup step it already performs is now also where the id comes from. **Declined**: keeping the phone as the input behind a lookup-stable derived column (`phone_erased_from_hash`) — a second permanent derivation of the number this endpoint exists to destroy, to save the caller one field it already holds.

One transaction, one `tenant_session`:

0. `SELECT pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext(:tenant_id))` as the **first statement** (D18). `create_booking` (`service.py:262`) and `reschedule` both take it, and `CustomersRepository.upsert`'s docstring is explicit that it is "safe to call without its own lock **because every caller already holds the per-tenant advisory lock**". Without it: erase reads the customer and passes the step-1 guard; a concurrent storefront `create_booking` — holding the lock erase did not take — upserts the same row and issues `existing.name = <her real name>`; that UPDATE blocks on erase's row lock and then applies *after* erase commits. Final state: `erased_at` set, `phone = 'erased:…'`, and her real name back on the row, plus a `confirmed` future booking that step 1's 409 exists to prevent. An erasure record that is a lie is worse than a refusal. One line makes the guard and the scrub sets actually atomic against the public booking path.
1. Refuse if she holds a `confirmed` booking with `starts_at > now()` → **409 `SUBJECT_HAS_ACTIVE_BOOKING`**. The erasure duty yields to performing the contract she is still party to; the message tells the owner to cancel or complete it first. Silently erasing a bride with a fitting on Thursday would break the appointment and the SMS link at once.
2. `customers`: `name = '[erased]'`, `phone = 'erased:{customer_id}'`, `erased_at = now()`, and

   ```sql
   marketing_consent_withdrawn_at = CASE
     WHEN marketing_consent_at IS NOT NULL THEN now()
     ELSE marketing_consent_withdrawn_at END
   ```

   **The `CASE` is load-bearing and the first draft's parenthetical inverted the mechanic.** `customers_marketing_withdraw_check` does not *enforce* "only when a consent exists" — it **rejects the write**. An unconditional `marketing_consent_withdrawn_at = now()` against a row with `marketing_consent_at IS NULL` raises `IntegrityError`, and a NULL consent is the *majority* case: every customer who never ticked the box. As drafted, this endpoint 500s for most subjects while the test plan — which sets up a consenting customer — stays green.

   The phone placeholder is per-row so `idx_customers_tenant_phone_unique` (`0008:47`) admits any number of erasures. **It is not "structurally un-sendable", and the first draft's reason for saying so was false against the code**: nothing on the send path calls `normalize_israeli_mobile`. `BookingCommsService._customer_phone` (`comms.py:477-494`) returns `customers.phone` verbatim and its own docstring says "this is a read and never a re-normalization", and `NotificationService.send_sms` (`service.py:86-114`) validates only `is_configured` before handing the string to the adapter. What actually blocks a send today is F15's confirmed-AND-future `_guard_live` (`owner.py:795-819`) — which is exactly what F50 is chartered to widen, and F20's spec would be the authority a later author cited for an invariant that was never true. **So F20 makes the claim true instead of restating it**: one `erased:`-prefix rejection at the top of `send_sms`, which is the single writer of `message_log` and the one place every caller routes through. One guard in the shared function is a smaller diff than a guard in every caller, and it survives F50 by construction.
3. `bookings` for that customer: `notes = NULL`, `manage_token_hash = NULL`. **The full surviving set, enumerated so it is a decision and not an omission**: `id`, `customer_id`, `appointment_type_id`, `starts_at`, `status`, `seat_index`, `dress_id`, `dress_name`, `dress_size`, `appointment_type_name`, `terms_version_accepted`, `terms_accepted_at`, `attendance_confirmed_at`, `cancelled_at`, `cancelled_by`, `created_at`. These are the boutique's business and tax record plus the terms-acceptance evidence. NULLing the token hash is what kills the still-live SMS link.

   The first draft asserted these survivors "identify nobody once the customer is scrubbed". With `customer_id` written into `audit_log.details` (step 6, and it must be — it is the only thing that makes an Amendment-13 complaint answerable, per `owner.py:730-740`'s own reasoning), that claim needs stating precisely: **the surviving row carries no name, no phone and no free text, and is re-identifiable only by an actor who can already read `audit_log`** — the owner and the platform operator, both of whom held the data before the erasure. It is not anonymous data; it is de-identified business record with a controlled re-identification key. Section 2 of the compliance record says exactly that, in those words, and the survivor list above is what it enumerates.
4. `message_log`: `phone = 'erased:{customer_id}'`, `body = ''`, matched by **`phone = :phone` OR `booking_id IN (:her_bookings)`** — where `:phone` is the value **read at step 1 and held in Python**, since step 2 has already overwritten the column by the time this runs. (Under the first draft's phone-keyed route the value arrived in the request body and this was free; keying on the id, D17, makes the read explicit. Same for steps 5's `otp_codes` predicate.) The `OR` is not belt-and-braces: F15's phone correction re-points `bookings.customer_id` at an existing customer row and **orphans the old rows** (F53's LOOP-STATE note names exactly this), so a phone-only predicate would leave her old number in the log. Residual: an OTP sent to a number that never produced a booking is reachable by neither predicate and is left to the 24-month `message_log` clock. Accepted, recorded as Risk 5.
5. `otp_codes` for that phone and `scheduled_messages` for her bookings: **purged**, not scrubbed — the whole row is her data.
6. `audit_log`: one row, `action = 'privacy_subject_erased'`, `actor_id` = the owner's staff id, `details = {"customer_id": …, "phone_last4": …, "counts": {…}, "reason": …}`. The export writes its own row, `action = 'privacy_subject_exported'`, same shape minus the counts — the first draft named only the erase action and left the read unaudited, which is checklist row 38's "**data access** by operators", not just mutations.

**The audit trail must not defeat the erasure it records (D19).** The first draft contradicted itself: step 6 said "field names and counts only, never values", while the Data-subject-rights section said every invocation is audited "with the actor and **the phone**". `audit_log` is explicitly exempt from every retention class **forever** (that exemption is correct and stays), so a full phone written there is a permanent copy of the one identifier this endpoint exists to destroy — in the one table designed never to be erased. The shipped house pattern is already the opposite: `owner.py:89-92` `_last4` — *"The most of a number that may be written down"* — and `correct_phone` stores only `old_phone_last4`/`new_phone_last4`. F20 adopts `_last4` for both subject routes. Four digits plus a `customer_id` is enough to answer a complaint and not enough to be a phone book.

**`reason` is capped and its content is constrained.** It is unbounded operator free text on a route whose whole subject is a named person, so it will predictably read «רונית ביקשה שימחקו אותה, 050-…». `MAX_ERASE_REASON_BYTES = 500` (see the constants table — deliberately two orders of magnitude below the text caps, because this field is prose about a decision, not a document), and the manage-app field carries a hint stating that `reason` must record *why*, never *who*. A test asserts the cap and asserts that no subject phone or name appears in the written `details`, the same way the export's `FORBIDDEN_KEYS` check is already planned.

Idempotent, and now actually reachable: a second call **with the same `customer_id`** finds `erased_at` set and returns the same summary with zero counts. Not a 409 — the owner tapping twice is not an error, and this is the same "she will click it more than once" argument F16 D1 made for the manage token. An *unknown* id is a 404, and an unknown phone 404s one step earlier at the export lookup, so "done" and "never existed" are never confused.

All three subject routes take a modest per-tenant `FixedWindowRateLimiter` **instance** each (house rule: budgets never share instances — `.memory/patterns` and F16 D-notes both say so). Neither is a defence in the OTP sense: an authenticated owner can already read every booking in her console. They are runaway brakes on a route that assembles a whole person into one document, and on one that destroys data irreversibly.

### Marketing consent (D6)

`BookingCreateRequest` gains `marketing_consent: bool = False`. The writer is **not** `upsert` (D20): it is one guarded statement, `CustomersRepository.record_marketing_consent(session, tenant_id, customer_id)` —

```sql
UPDATE customers SET marketing_consent_at = now(), marketing_consent_source = 'booking_form'
 WHERE tenant_id = :t AND id = :id AND deleted_at IS NULL AND marketing_consent_at IS NULL
```

— called from `create_booking` on **whichever customer row the request resolved**, under the advisory lock the service already holds. Semantics:

- ticked, and `marketing_consent_at IS NULL` → stamped by the `WHERE` clause itself, atomically.
- ticked, and a consent already exists → the `WHERE` matches nothing. The original timestamp is the evidence; re-stamping it would move the proof of when she agreed.
- **unticked → the statement is not issued at all.** An empty checkbox on a later booking is not a withdrawal. Withdrawal must be an affirmative act, and treating an omission as one would silently revoke a consent the boutique can prove it holds.

**Why a separate writer rather than a flag on `upsert`, and why the first draft's reasoning was wrong about the code.** It claimed the 0009 idempotent-replay path "must not re-stamp: `created` is false there, and `upsert`'s 'only when NULL' rule makes the replay a no-op regardless". In fact `service.py:296` returns `BookingClaim(booking=replayed, created=False, manage_token=None)` **before** `upsert` is ever reached at `:313`, so on that path the writer is not merely a no-op — it is **unreachable**. Reachable consequence: a customer who already holds an appointment at instant T (capacity ≥ 2, or any re-submission of a slot she holds) walks the form again, ticks the marketing box, receives a 201, and **no consent is recorded anywhere**. A consent is an affirmative act independent of whether a booking is new, so silently dropping it is not a defensible default; and dropping it *because of statement order* is not a decision at all. `existing_customer` is already in hand at `:287`, so the new writer is called on both paths — the replay branch and the normal one — and the two-call-sites cost is the price of the consent surviving a replay. Tested on the replay path explicitly.

`bookings` gains nothing. Checklist row 40 wants "timestamp + terms-version + source": the *policy* consent already carries timestamp + version on the booking, and the *marketing* consent carries timestamp + source on the customer, which is where it belongs because the consent is to be messaged as a person, not for one appointment. A third column recording which booking produced it has no reader; declined.

**A consent taken with no way to withdraw it is not a consent (D15).** In the first draft the *only* writer of `marketing_consent_withdrawn_at` was the erase endpoint — so the sole way for a bride to stop marketing she has not yet received was to have her entire record scrubbed, and the omission appeared in neither Out of scope nor the Risk register. The withdrawal right attaches when the consent is taken, not when the first message is sent. `POST /manage/privacy/marketing-withdraw` body `{"customer_id": "…"}` → one statement:

```sql
UPDATE customers SET marketing_consent_withdrawn_at = now()
 WHERE tenant_id = :t AND id = :id AND deleted_at IS NULL
   AND marketing_consent_at IS NOT NULL AND marketing_consent_withdrawn_at IS NULL
```

The `WHERE` keeps `customers_marketing_withdraw_check` unreachable (same lesson as the erase `CASE`) and makes a double-tap a 200 with `changed: false`. The subject id comes from the same export/lookup step the erase uses. One route, one statement, no new table and no new surface — and it is what makes checklist row 40's opt-out clause *capturable* rather than only *sendable*, which is the half F20 can honestly discharge. The withdrawal method must also be **stated in the collection notice copy** (§30A); the exact Hebrew sentence is counsel's and goes to the user with the rest of the copy. **Who may invoke it was Gate 1 question 4, and the user OVERRULED the spec's safe default: it ships `(OWNER, SHIFT_MANAGER)`.** The spec proposed OWNER-only to match every other privacy mutation; the ruling is that recording a phoned-in opt-out is routine front-desk work and the person who answers the phone must be able to honour it immediately, which is better for the subject and for §30A response time. Concretely this route carries **no** `dependencies=[Depends(require_role(StaffRole.OWNER))]` — it inherits the boutique router's own `(OWNER, SHIFT_MANAGER)` gate, and it is therefore the one privacy route absent from `OWNER_ONLY` in `test_staff_role_gating.py`. It remains the *least* destructive privacy action (one nullable timestamp, idempotent, no data lost), which is what makes the widening defensible where the export and erase routes stay owner-only.

The checkbox sits on the **`details` step**, directly under the collection-notice block and two navigations away from the required terms checkbox on the `terms` step — so unbundling is *structural*, not a styling promise. Declined: placing it on `verify` beside the phone (the channel it authorises, which is the better semantic argument) — `verify` is the flow's most failure-prone screen, carrying OTP retries, resend budgets and two dead-end states, and it does not get a new optional control.

### The retention job (D7, D8, D9)

New module `app/privacy/retention.py`. A **registry of policies**, because the brief requires reusable machinery and three future consumers are already named (F38 scrub, F33 queue-ticket purge, and F53's notes/tags inherit the customer classes). Two actions, both with a live F20 consumer so neither ships as speculative code:

- **PURGE** — hard `DELETE`. For classes where the row *is* the personal data.
- **SCRUB** — `UPDATE` blanking the personal columns, row retained. For classes that are business or operational records containing personal data. This is the epic brief's own words: *"PII-scrub job (anonymize in place — soft-delete is not erasure)"*.

```python
@dataclasses.dataclass(frozen=True)
class RetentionPolicy:
    name: str                       # audit suffix + the Settings key it reads
    action: RetentionAction         # PURGE | SCRUB
    run: Callable[[AsyncSession, UUID, datetime, int], Awaitable[int]]
                                    # (session, tenant_id, now, chunk) -> rows touched

POLICIES: tuple[RetentionPolicy, ...] = (...)   # ORDER IS LOAD-BEARING, see below
```

The runner owns the loop, so no policy repeats it: call `run` until it returns fewer than `CHUNK_SIZE` rows or `MAX_CHUNKS` is reached (`CHUNK_SIZE = 500`, `MAX_CHUNKS = 50`, `backfill.py:36-38`). Every policy executes inside that tenant's `tenant_session` — `scheduled_messages` was not allowed to be the codebase's first RLS exception (F16 D6) and a job that hard-deletes is emphatically not going to be either. One tenant's failure is logged and skipped, never allowed to stop the others (`worker.py:63-72`'s reasoning, and here the stakes are higher: a single bad row must not stall every boutique's retention).

**The retention runner gets its own tenant enumeration, and must NOT reuse `list_active()` (D21).** The first draft said "tenants are enumerated exactly as `poll_once` does"; `worker.py:60` calls `TenantsRepository.list_active()`, which filters `deleted_at IS NULL AND status = 'active'` (`tenants.py:97-104`). Both excluded states are reachable and operator-invoked — `suspend()` and `soft_delete()` are shipped CLI operations. So a boutique suspended for non-payment, or churned and off-boarded, would keep every bride's name, phone, free-text notes and every SMS body **forever, with no clock ever applied** — which makes the Problem statement's own charge ("nothing is ever deleted") permanently true for precisely the boutiques most likely to hold abandoned data. Under Amendment 13 the retention duty does not lapse when the controller's account does, and this spec's own words are that "a boutique may not choose its own retention for a duty the platform enforces on its behalf" — suspension must not be a way to opt out of it either.

New one-method addition beside it: `TenantsRepository.list_all()` — **every tenant row, no status filter and no `deleted_at` filter** — used only by the retention runner. `list_active()` keeps the SMS poller, where skipping a suspended tenant is correct (a suspended boutique should not be texting). Whether off-boarding should additionally trigger an *accelerated terminal purge* is a bigger policy than a clock and is out of scope (recorded in Out of scope and Risk 11); running the ordinary per-class clocks for those tenants is strictly better than the status quo and needs no decision to be right.

The five classes of pre-decided #10, plus the two the code forces:

| Order | Class | Table | Clock | Action | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `otp_codes` | `otp_codes` | `created_at` + `retention_otp_seconds` (15 min) | PURGE | whole row |
| 2 | `sessions` | `sessions` | `expires_at < now()` OR `deleted_at IS NOT NULL` | PURGE | whole row. **No new setting** — pre-decided #10 says "at existing TTL", and the existing TTL is already `session_ttl_seconds`, written into `expires_at` at mint time. A second knob would be a second source of truth. |
| 3 | `message_log` | `message_log` | `created_at` + `retention_message_log_seconds` (24 months) | PURGE | whole row |
| 4 | `bookings` | `bookings` | `starts_at` + `retention_bookings_seconds` (7 years) | PURGE | whole row, **and** `scheduled_messages` for the same booking ids in the same statement batch (pre-decided #10: "scheduled messages purged with their booking"). An orphan sweep over `scheduled_messages` was declined — it is an anti-join per tick to reach rows the booking purge already knows the ids of. |
| 5 | `customers` | `customers` | orphaned by 4, **and** `created_at` + `retention_orphan_customer_seconds` (30 days), **and `erased_at IS NULL`** | **SCRUB** | `name`/`phone` placeholders + `erased_at`, row retained |

**Order 4 before 5 is load-bearing** and is asserted by a test on the tuple, not left to a comment: the customer scrub's feed is "no live booking points at me", which only becomes true after the booking purge runs.

**Every policy's predicate must be falsified by its own action, and the registry asserts it rather than trusting each author (D22).** The first draft's `customers` feed was "orphaned **and** older than 30 days" with no `erased_at IS NULL` — and a row already carrying the placeholders still satisfies both, so the policy re-scrubbed its own output on every tick. Three consequences, all silent: `erased_at` — the erasure evidence, and for a §14 subject whose bookings have since been purged, the *proof* of her erasure — is overwritten hourly; with ≥ 500 already-scrubbed customers the runner returns a full page every pass and burns all 50 chunks (25 000 pointless UPDATEs per tenant per tick); and under a stable ordering it never reaches the not-yet-scrubbed rows queued behind them, so the policy stops doing its job entirely at exactly the point it has enough work to matter. Both of the first draft's listed tests pass throughout.

The `erased_at IS NULL` predicate fixes this policy. The **invariant** is what stops the next one repeating it: `test_retention_policies.py` asserts, for every registered policy, that a **second consecutive run against the same fixture touches zero rows**. F38's staff SCRUB (pre-decided #34/#35) inherits this policy shape verbatim, so the guard has to live in the registry, not in one policy's comment.

**Why customers are scrubbed and not purged.** `bookings.customer_id` is a no-FK pointer (house rule), and F22 (waitlist entries), F33 (queue tickets) and F53 (notes/tags) will all point at customers too. A purged customer leaves those dangling with no way to distinguish "erased" from "never existed"; a scrubbed one keeps a resolvable id carrying no personal data — which is precisely the shape pre-decided #35 already blesses for staff ("soft-delete the staff record, retain operational history, scheduled scrub blanks personal fields"). One mechanism, two consumers, and F38 inherits a SCRUB implementation with tests instead of writing one.

**Why the 30-day orphan grace exists, and it is not padding.** A customer row can only be created inside `create_booking`, so the sole way one becomes orphaned early is F15's phone correction: `CustomersRepository.set_phone`'s docstring records that when the corrected number already belongs to another live customer, the service "re-points `bookings.customer_id` at the existing row instead" — which instantly orphans the row it left. Without a grace, a mistaken correction would be anonymised within the hour and unrecoverable. 30 days is an undo window; it is also why the constant is named after the orphan case rather than after the customer.

**Exempt by design, and asserted by test:** `terms_versions` (immutable evidence pinned by every booking; `app_user` holds no DELETE — a policy naming it would fail at the grant, so the test asserts no policy names it), `platform_audit_log` (INSERT-only, not even readable), `audit_log` (the evidence that retention ran — giving it a retention class would eventually erase the proof of the erasures, and checklist row 38 exists to prevent exactly that), `tenants`, `staff_users` (F38's, per pre-decided #34/#35), and the catalog/availability tables (no personal data).

**Where it runs.** One `if` in `worker.main()`'s existing loop, guarded on a `next_retention_at` timestamp and `settings.retention_enabled`. Declined: a second Railway service (a process to run an hourly DELETE), a scheduler dependency (APScheduler for one comparison), and `asyncio.gather` of two loops (two concurrent DB workloads in an unsupervised process, for no benefit at pilot volume). The reminder poller keeps its 60-second cadence; retention gets `retention_poll_interval_seconds`, default 3600.

**Also invocable from the CLI**, with `--dry-run`: `python -m app.cli retention --operator NAME [--dry-run]`. Dry-run counts what each policy *would* touch and issues no write, which is how an operator inspects the first run against real data before trusting the scheduled one. The invocation writes one `platform_audit_log` row (`PlatformAuditAction.RETENTION_RUN`) naming the operator — checklist row 12's discipline, and `PlatformAuditLogRepository.record` already generates the id client-side because that table has no SELECT (`app/platform/repository.py:22-26`), so nothing new is needed. The per-tenant `audit_log` rows the job writes are the tenant's own evidence and are a separate trail with a separate purpose. **A row is written only when something was actually touched** — five rows per tenant per hour of "deleted 0" is permanent bloat in the one table that has no retention class.

**`--operator` is `required=True` on this subcommand only, deliberately diverging from `_with_operator` (D23).** `cli.py:43` defaults it to `os.environ.get("USER", "operator")` and `:47-50` applies that helper to all five shipped subcommands, so the first draft's test assertion ("`--operator` required") would have failed against the reused helper. The divergence is the right way to resolve that, not the assertion: `provision`, `suspend`, `reset-password`, `list` and `backfill` are recoverable or read-only, and this one issues an irreversible multi-tenant hard-DELETE. `$USER` on a shared box is not an audit identity. `retention` therefore declares its own `--operator` with `required=True` and a comment naming the reason; the helper is untouched for everything else.

### Data-subject rights (D10, D11)

The honest answer, stated plainly: **an owner-invoked export and erase action on the manage console, plus a documented manual process** — there is no self-service channel, because the customer has no account until F24's client portal.

- **Access** → `POST /manage/privacy/subject-export`. The document is downloaded as JSON by the manage app and handed to the subject by the boutique.
- **Correction** → **already shipped, partially, and the gap is named.** F15's `POST /manage/bookings/{id}/phone` corrects a number, and `CustomersRepository.upsert` updates `name` on every new booking ("she typed it on this booking, so it is the most recent thing she calls herself"). What cannot be corrected today: a name, without her making a new booking; and `bookings.notes`, at all. F53's customers CRM is the surface that closes both. No new correction endpoint here — building a second customer-edit surface weeks before F53 ships one is the un-lazy thing. Recorded as Risk 4 with F53 as the trigger.
- **Erasure** → `POST /manage/privacy/subject-erase`.
- **Identity verification is the boutique's step, not the platform's.** The platform's controls are that only an owner can invoke either action, that every invocation — export *and* erase — is audited with the actor, the `customer_id` and the phone's **last four digits only** (D19, never the full number), and that the procedure document tells the owner how to verify a requester before acting. A platform that verified identity itself would be acting as controller, which it is not (`architecture.md:20`).
- **The 30-day statutory clock is procedural, not enforced in code.** No reminder, no ticket queue, no deadline field. The procedure document carries it. Declined: a request-tracking table — nothing in v1 can even receive a request electronically, so the table would have exactly one writer (the owner, by hand) and one reader (the owner). Recorded, with F24 as the natural trigger for building one.

| `MAX_PRIVACY_TEXT_BYTES` | 8 × 1024 | `app/privacy/validation.py` | byte-precise because Hebrew is 2 bytes/char. **Not** the `MAX_TERMS_TEXT_BYTES` 50 KB precedent — that text lives in its own `terms_versions` table behind `GET /storefront/terms`, and this text lives on `tenants.settings`, which `RepositoryTenantResolver` reads **in middleware on every non-exempt request** (`resolver.py:8-20`, no cache until E5; `settings` is a plain eager `mapped_column(JSONB)`, `models/tenant.py:20-22`; `EXEMPT_PATHS` is `/health` + docs only). Two 50 KB fields would mean ~100 KB of JSONB read and deserialised on every anonymous catalog paint, every OTP send and every `/manage` call — and the storefront read throttle cannot bound it, because it is a router dependency and the middleware has already done the read by the time a 429 fires. A §11 notice is ~1.5 KB; 8 KB is generous. **The next feature that eyes `settings` for bulk text should read this row first.** |
| `MAX_ERASE_REASON_BYTES` | 500 | ditto | operator prose about a decision, not a document — and it lands in `audit_log`, the one table exempt from every retention class (D19) |
| `ERASED_NAME` | `"[erased]"` | `app/privacy/retention.py` | ASCII, one LTR run needing no bidi care in an RTL console. Declined: exposing `erased_at` on F15's `OwnerBookingRow` plus new manage copy — two schema changes and a frontend change to say what one literal already says |
| `ERASED_PHONE_PREFIX` | `"erased:"` | ditto | per-row uniqueness under `idx_customers_tenant_phone_unique`, and un-sendable through `normalize_israeli_mobile` |
| `CHUNK_SIZE` / `MAX_CHUNKS` | 500 / 50 | ditto | `backfill.py:36-38` |
| `retention_enabled` | **`False`** | `Settings` | a kill switch for an unattended hard-delete job, and it ships **off** (D9 revised, Risk 2) |
| `retention_poll_interval_seconds` | 3600 | `Settings` | |
| `retention_otp_seconds` | `15 * 60` | `Settings` | pre-decided #10. Exactly `OTP_TTL_SECONDS + VERIFICATION_TOKEN_TTL_SECONDS`; safe only because verify refuses at `expires_at <= now`, so no token can be alive at `created_at + 900`. **Zero margin — see the floors** |
| `retention_message_log_seconds` | `730 * 24 * 3600` | `Settings` | 24 months |
| `retention_bookings_seconds` | `365 * 7 * 24 * 3600` | `Settings` | 7 years |
| `retention_orphan_customer_seconds` | `30 * 24 * 3600` | `Settings` | the phone-correction undo window |

**Retention periods live in `Settings`, and the house rule against product policy in `Settings` is addressed rather than ignored.** `config.py:39-43` and `notifications/validation.py:1-4` put product policy in a validation module because a byte cap in env can drift out of sync with a DB CHECK and a frontend constant, producing an `IntegrityError` on a user action. Retention periods have no DB CHECK and no frontend twin, so that failure mode is unreachable; what they do have is pre-decided #10's explicit "tunable settings" and #34's "changeable in one line", and a counsel confirmation at F21 that should change one env var for every tenant at once. Declined: per-tenant retention in `settings.privacy` — a boutique may not choose its own retention for a duty the platform enforces on its behalf; and hardcoded constants — #10 forbids them.

**`retention_enabled` ships `False`, and that reverses the first draft (D9 revised).** It defaulted to `True` on the stated grounds that "default-off would leave four checklist rows red while looking green in code" — compliance optics, not safety — while this spec's own Risk 2 says, in bold, that `RETENTION_ENABLED=false` in production is the honest posture until a tested restore exists. Those cannot both ship. What default-on actually deploys is an **unattended, irreversible, chunked mass-DELETE** across `otp_codes`, `sessions`, `message_log`, `bookings` and `scheduled_messages`, on clocks read from environment variables, against a database with **no backup and no tested restore** — `security-checklist-v1.md:44` is unchecked and is F21's. A default that has to be un-set by a deploy note to be safe is a loaded gun with a sticker on it; row 42 of the checklist is honestly amber (see the accounting table above) and F21's backup row is the gate that flips both. The job still ships complete and fully tested — turning it on is one env var, and the CLI `--dry-run` is how the first real run is inspected before that happens.

**A boot validator with per-class floors**, because a fat-fingered env var here is unrecoverable data loss and it is the only place in this codebase where that is true. The first draft's floors caught only the literal `=0` case they named — every one sat 15×–730× below the default it guarded, so the realistic fat-finger, a **dropped digit**, sailed through: `RETENTION_BOOKINGS_SECONDS=220752000` mistyped as `22075200` is 255 days, comfortably above a 30-day floor, and the next tick hard-DELETEs every booking older than 255 days *including* `terms_version_accepted`/`terms_accepted_at` — the very evidence the erase design goes out of its way to preserve. Each floor is therefore set within an order of magnitude of the default it guards, so a single dropped digit always trips it:

| Setting | Default | Floor | Digit-drop lands at |
|---|---|---|---|
| `retention_otp_seconds` | 900 | **`OTP_TTL_SECONDS + VERIFICATION_TOKEN_TTL_SECONDS`** (= 900) | 90 → rejected |
| `retention_message_log_seconds` | 730 d | 180 d | 73 d → rejected |
| `retention_bookings_seconds` | 7 y | 3 y | 255 d → rejected |
| `retention_orphan_customer_seconds` | 30 d | 7 d | 3 d → rejected |
| `retention_poll_interval_seconds` | 3600 | 60 | — |

The OTP floor is **imported from `app/notifications/validation.py`, not written as a number**, so a future TTL change moves the floor with it. It also equals the default, which is correct and intentional: 15 minutes is exactly sufficient (see "Where the interview and the code disagree"), so there is nothing below it to allow. The first draft's `≥ 60` would have let `RETENTION_OTP_SECONDS=60` boot clean and purge every OTP row a minute after send — every customer's `POST /storefront/otp/verify` would then find no live code, no booking could complete, and there would be no error anywhere to explain it. A platform-wide availability outage that passes the validator whose entire purpose is to prevent it.

`test_config.py` covers the digit-drop case for every class, not just `=0`. The `_forbid_sms_test_paths_in_production` validator is the precedent for failing at boot rather than degrading.

### Errors

| Error | Status | Code |
|---|---|---|
| Unknown phone, or already-erased subject, on **export** | 404 | `SUBJECT_NOT_FOUND` |
| Unknown `customer_id` on erase or marketing-withdraw | 404 | `SUBJECT_NOT_FOUND` |
| Erase with a `confirmed` future booking | 409 | `SUBJECT_HAS_ACTIVE_BOOKING` |
| Notice/DPA over the byte cap, non-string, unknown key, **or either key omitted** | 400 / 422 | `VALIDATION_ERROR` (house handler, F7) |
| `reason` over `MAX_ERASE_REASON_BYTES` | 400 | `VALIDATION_ERROR` |
| Malformed phone on export | 400 | `VALIDATION_ERROR` |
| `shift_manager` on `PUT /manage/privacy` or any of the three subject routes | 403 | `NOT_AUTHORIZED` (F31's literal-pinned contract) |
| Subject-route budget exhausted | 429 | `TOO_MANY_ATTEMPTS` |

A second erase of an already-erased subject **by the same `customer_id`** is a **200** with zero counts, not a 404 — the row exists and the outcome she asked for holds. This is reachable only because the erase is keyed on the id (D17); keyed on the phone it was not reachable at all, and the 200 would have had to double as the answer for "no such person", which is how a mistyped digit becomes a fabricated erasure record.

## Adversarial-review findings raised and REJECTED

Recorded because a silently dropped finding is indistinguishable from an oversight to the next reader.

1. **"The 'write an audit row only when something was touched' mitigation does not bound the growth it was introduced to bound."** *(MINOR, CORRECTNESS lens.)* The claim: `otp_codes` is purged hourly and every booking attempt creates an `otp_codes` row, so nearly every tick is non-zero → ~8 800 permanent `audit_log` rows per tenant per year in the one table with no retention class; the proposed fix was to collapse a tick into one row, or to stop auditing `otp_codes`/`sessions` entirely.

   **Rejected on both the arithmetic and the premise.** The arithmetic assumes an OTP row in nearly every one of the 8 760 hourly ticks in a year, which requires ~24 booking attempts per boutique per day, sustained around the clock — an order of magnitude above pilot volume, and spread across business hours besides. A boutique taking five bookings a day produces a non-zero purge in roughly five ticks a day, not twenty-four: ~1 800 rows a year, ~18 000 over a decade, in a table Postgres will not notice. More importantly the premise inverts what the mitigation was for: it was introduced to stop rows accruing with **the clock** (five per tenant per hour whether or not anything happened, forever), and it does exactly that — after it, rows scale with *activity*, which is the only thing an audit trail should scale with. A trail that records real destruction events is not bloat; it is the artifact. Risk 10 already records the "small permanent rows accumulate" shape for scrubbed customer shells, and this has the same non-driver.

   The second half of the proposed fix would also cost something real: dropping `otp_codes`/`sessions` from `audit_log` means the per-tenant evidence no longer shows that the fastest clock ever ran, which is the class a regulator is most likely to ask about. Kept as drafted.

## Risks & open items

1. **The platform default is not lawyer-reviewed, and the pilot will go live on it.** Interview Q8 chose this knowingly as "the only option that leaves the pilot legal on day one", and the mitigation is structural: swapping in counsel's text edits one settings field per tenant, or one constant for every tenant that has not overridden. The disclaimer is shown to the controller and not to the data subject. *Owner: user. Trigger: the standing `user_actions` counsel review, before pilot go-live.*
2. **The retention job hard-deletes real customer data, unattended, on a clock read from the environment.** Bounded by: per-class boot floors sized to catch a dropped digit (D9), **`retention_enabled` defaulting to `False` so it does not run at all until someone decides it should**, chunked transactions with `MAX_CHUNKS`, a CLI `--dry-run` for the first real inspection, per-tenant audit rows, and `audit_log` itself being exempt so the evidence survives what it records. Unbounded by: any backup, because there is none yet — checklist row 44 is F21's and it is red. The first draft resolved this contradiction the wrong way, defaulting the flag **on** and relying on a deploy note to turn it off; the flag now ships off and the deploy note is redundant rather than load-bearing. *Owner: team + user (Gate 1 question 2). Trigger: F21's backup/restore row going green is what makes enabling it safe.*
3. **The notice is not versioned, so the exact text shown at a past collection is not reconstructable.** `terms_versions` is versioned because it carries money; this does not, and Q8 put it in settings. Upgrade path if a regulator or counsel disagrees: an append-only `privacy_notices` table plus a `bookings.privacy_notice_version` pin, minted in the booking transaction exactly as `terms_version_accepted` already is — one migration and one column, reversible into. *Owner: user (counsel's call). Trigger: F21 audit, or counsel's text arriving.*
4. **Correction is only half-served.** A phone can be corrected (F15); a name cannot without a new booking, and `bookings.notes` cannot be corrected at all — while the export endpoint hands the subject a document containing both, which is precisely how she will discover an error she then cannot have fixed. Manual workaround today: the owner cancels and rebooks, or annotates. *Owner: team. Trigger: F53 (customers CRM), which ships notes and a customer edit surface.*
5. **Erasure has two reachable residues.** (a) An OTP `message_log` row for a number that never produced a booking is matched by neither erase predicate and waits out the 24-month clock. (b) F15's phone correction orphans `message_log` rows; the `OR booking_id IN (…)` predicate reaches them **only** while the booking still exists, so a correction on a booking later purged at 7 years leaves the old row to its own clock. Both are bounded in time and neither is reachable by a name. Accepted. *Owner: team. Trigger: F21 audit re-derives.*
6. **F29's tenant-scoped config cache (pre-decided #21) will stale the public notice.** `RepositoryTenantResolver` reads `tenants` per request today, so an override is live immediately; the moment settings are cached, a boutique that publishes a corrected privacy notice will keep serving the old one for the TTL. The privacy key must be named in F29's invalidation scope. *Owner: team. Trigger: F29 spec.*
7. **The sub-processor list is a live document with no mechanical guard.** Every feature that adds an external data processor — F17/F18 (Grow), F46 (Twilio WhatsApp), F47 (Cloudflare Stream), and the SMS adapter itself — must amend both `PLATFORM_SUBPROCESSORS_HE` and `.planning/ppl-compliance-record.md`. Nothing in CI can detect a missed amendment. **What D14 does fix is the propagation half**: because the list is platform-owned and un-overridable, one constant edit reaches every tenant including those that overrode their DPA prose. The first draft had it inside the overridable blob, so an amendment would have reached only the boutiques that never customised — meaning the moment a real SMS provider went live, every *overriding* boutique's public disclosure became factually false, permanently and silently. That half is now structural; remembering to make the edit at all is still human. *Owner: team. Trigger: any feature adding an external processor; F21's audit is the backstop.*
8. **The worker still has no liveness signal** (F16 Risk 5, unchanged) and now runs a second job in the same loop. A retention run wedged inside a chunk loop silences every boutique's reminders with no alert. `MAX_CHUNKS` bounds the work but not a hung connection. *Owner: team. Trigger: F21's hardening pass.*
9. **The retention job's per-tick cost is O(tenants × policies)** — seven statements per tenant per hour, two of them per-tenant seq scans (`otp_codes`, `scheduled_messages`). Noise at pilot volume; the same ceiling F16's poller already carries and the same place it gets revisited. *Owner: team. Trigger: F29's scale pass.*
10. **Scrubbed customer rows accumulate permanently** — a ~100-byte anonymous shell per customer who passed the 7-year mark, retained so future pointers (F22/F33/F53) resolve. No growth driver exists at pilot scale; if one ever does, the shells are purgeable in one statement because by then nothing points at them. *Owner: team. Trigger: none scheduled; recorded so it is a decision and not a surprise.*
11. **Off-boarding does not accelerate the clock.** D21 makes retention run for suspended and soft-deleted tenants, so their data is no longer frozen forever — but it ages out on the ordinary per-class periods, which for `bookings` is seven years after a boutique has stopped existing as a customer. Whether off-boarding should trigger a terminal purge is a tenant-lifecycle decision with a surface F20 does not own, and it is out of scope here. *Owner: user (a data-retention policy question, not an engineering one). Trigger: the first real off-boarding, or F21's audit.*
12. **`audit_log.details` is a controlled re-identification key for erased subjects, and that is deliberate.** D19 cuts it to `customer_id` + `phone_last4`, which is the shipped `owner.py` posture and enough to answer an Amendment-13 complaint; it is not zero. An actor who can read `audit_log` can walk `customer_id` to a scrubbed customer's surviving bookings (`dress_size`, `dress_name`, `starts_at`). Only the owner and the platform operator can, and both held the full data before the erasure — but the compliance record must describe the survivors as *de-identified with a controlled key*, not as *anonymous*, because the second word would be false. *Owner: team. Trigger: F21 audit.*

## Decisions Log

- **D1 — Three shipped collection points (booking `details`, OTP send, booking create), not four.** The catalog enquiry the brief names does not exist in the code; the storefront read router is GET-only and no contact form is registered anywhere. Codebase-consistent reading taken and recorded above. Declined: building an enquiry form to have something to notice at (a new public PII intake, invented inside a compliance feature).
- **D2 — One notice placement, on the `details` step, discharges all three points.** The three are one linear flow and `details` is the earliest step at which any personal datum is typed. Declined: a notice per point (three copies of one legal text on one flow, and the `verify` step is the flow's most failure-prone screen — it gets no new content); and placing it on `verify` where the server first *receives* data (by then she has already typed her name and notes two screens back).
- **D3 — Both overridable documents live in `tenants.settings` under a `privacy` key, written through F7's atomic `||` merge** (which protects sibling top-level keys and *not* fields inside `privacy` — see D16, and D14 for the third document, which is not in settings at all). Interview Q8 and pre-decided #19, and `merge_settings`' docstring names this feature. Declined: a `privacy_texts` table (a row per tenant holding text a tenant usually does not override, plus a repository, plus a migration, to store what one JSONB key stores); and seeding the default into every tenant's settings at provisioning (twenty copies of one un-reviewed paragraph, and counsel's replacement then needs a data migration instead of a constant edit).
- **D4 — A blank override reverts to the platform default; a boutique cannot publish an empty notice.** `settings || patch` can add and replace a key but cannot remove one, so `""` is the only reachable revert sentinel — and the `profile_text` `""`-to-null collapse is the shipped precedent. Declined: a `notice_use_default: bool` flag beside the text (two fields that can disagree, and the disagreement is silent); and honouring an empty override literally (publishing a blank privacy page is the non-compliance the page exists to prevent — `AccessibilityPage.tsx:12-14`'s argument).
- **D5 — `dpa_text` is the boutique's processor-disclosure clause in its own ToS, not the platform↔boutique contract.** `architecture.md:20` fixes "DPA in ToS", and a boutique overriding the contract the platform offers it is incoherent — the only reading under which Q8's "overridable" parses. The platform default names today's real sub-processors only, and explicitly does **not** name Twilio, because `sms_provider` accepts `"fake"` or nothing (`config.py:56`). Declined: a per-tenant override of the platform's own processor obligations.
- **D6 — Marketing consent is `marketing_consent_at`/`_source`/`_withdrawn_at` on `customers`, captured by a checkbox on the `details` step.** Default-off is structural (a NULL timestamp, not a boolean with a flippable server default), withdrawal is additive so the evidence of a past consent survives, and the step separation makes unbundling structural rather than cosmetic. Declined: a boolean column (a server default is one migration away from becoming opt-out); clearing `marketing_consent_at` on withdrawal (destroys the Spam-Law defence); treating an unticked box on a later booking as a withdrawal (an omission is not an affirmative act); consent on `bookings` rather than `customers` (the consent is to be messaged as a person, and an opt-out must bind every future send); a `marketing_consent_booking_id` column (no reader); and the checkbox on `verify` beside the phone it authorises (the better semantics, on the worst screen). Superseded in two places by review: the writer is D20's guarded UPDATE, not `upsert`; and withdrawal gains a real writer of its own in D15, because "the erase endpoint is the withdrawal path" is not a withdrawal path.
- **D7 — Pre-decided #10's "OTP codes 15 minutes" is the row's retention clock, not the code's TTL.** `OTP_TTL_SECONDS = 300` and `VERIFICATION_TOKEN_TTL_SECONDS = 600` are unchanged. **Corrected from the first draft**: 15 minutes from `created_at` is *exactly* `300 + 600`, not "5 minutes past the longest live thing on the row" — the margin is zero. It is safe only because `service.py:292` refuses to verify at `expires_at <= now`, so no verification token can be alive at `created_at + 900`. The floor therefore equals the default and is **imported from `notifications/validation.py`** so a TTL change moves it. Declined: reading #10 as a TTL change (contradicts the epic's ≤5-minute requirement and checklist row 16); adding a tunable for either TTL (they are product policy, which `Settings` deliberately does not carry); and the first draft's `≥ 60` floor, which would let `RETENTION_OTP_SECONDS=60` boot clean and make every booking in the platform uncompletable with no error anywhere.
- **D8 — The retention job is a policy registry with two actions, PURGE and SCRUB, and both have a live F20 consumer.** The brief requires reusable machinery; F38 (staff scrub), F33 (queue-ticket purge) and F53 are named consumers. `customers` is the SCRUB consumer inside F20 itself, so SCRUB does not ship as untested spec-only code. Registry order is asserted by test, because the customer scrub's feed only exists after the booking purge. Declined: a one-off booking sweeper (the brief forbids it and F38 would have to invent SCRUB); a generic declarative policy (table + column + age) — three of the five classes need a predicate no such schema expresses; and purging customers instead of scrubbing them (dangling no-FK pointers from F22/F33/F53 with no way to tell erased from never-existed, against pre-decided #35's own shape for staff).
- **D9 (revised) — Retention periods live in `Settings` with per-class boot floors sized to catch a dropped digit, and `retention_enabled` ships `False`.** The house rule against product policy in `Settings` guards against drift from a DB CHECK or a frontend constant, neither of which exists here; pre-decided #10 and #34 both say "tunable settings". The floors exist because this is the only config in the repo where a typo is unrecoverable data loss — and the first draft's floors sat 15×–730× below their defaults, so they caught only the literal `=0` case they were written for while the realistic fat-finger (one digit short) passed. Each floor is now within an order of magnitude of its default. **The flag default is reversed**: the first draft shipped `True` on the stated grounds that default-off "would leave four checklist rows red while looking green in code" — that is compliance optics, and it directly contradicted this spec's own Risk 2. An unattended irreversible mass-DELETE against a database with no backup and no tested restore does not ship armed; the honest posture is the default, not a deploy note. F21's backup row is the gate that flips it, and Gate 1 question 2 is the user's chance to overrule. Declined: per-tenant retention overrides (a boutique may not choose its own retention for a platform-enforced duty); hardcoded constants (#10 forbids them); a second Railway service, a scheduler dependency, and `asyncio.gather` of two loops (all three buy nothing over one comparison in a loop that already exists); and an orphan sweep over `scheduled_messages` (an anti-join per tick to find rows the booking purge already has the ids of).
- **D10 — Data-subject rights are served by an owner-invoked export and erase on the manage console, plus a written procedure; identity verification and the 30-day clock are the boutique's.** She has no account until F24, so there is no self-service path to build. Correction reuses F15's phone endpoint and waits for F53 for names and notes. Declined: a customer-facing DSR form (a fourth anonymous PII intake, and it would make the platform verify identity — which would make it a controller, which `architecture.md:20` says it is not); a request-tracking table (one hand-writer, one reader); and a second customer-edit surface weeks before F53 ships one.
- **D11 — Erasure scrubs in place and refuses while a live future booking exists.** The enumerated survivor set (D-note in erase step 3) is the boutique's business record plus the terms-acceptance evidence; the token hash is NULLed so the SMS link dies; `message_log` is matched by phone **OR** her booking ids because F15's phone correction orphans rows. Refusal is a 409 telling the owner to cancel or complete first — the erasure duty yields to a contract she is still party to. A repeat erase is a 200, not a 409 (F16 D1's "she will click it more than once", applied to the owner) — reachable only because of D17. Declined: hard-deleting the bookings (destroys the tenant's tax and dispute record, and PPL erasure is about the personal data, not the row); `deleted_at` on the customer (soft delete is not erasure — the epic brief and `architecture.md:40` both say so); erasing through the retention registry (request-driven work forced into a clock-driven abstraction); and a `phone`-only `message_log` predicate (provably misses the pre-correction rows).

- **D12 — Three checklist rows go green at F20, not four; two go amber with a named owner.** Rows 39, 41 and 43 are fully discharged here. Row 40 keeps its send-time opt-out clause open (F46) and row 42 keeps its queue-entry class open (F33) *and* is not "running" while `retention_enabled` defaults off (D9). The first draft's "four rows go green" undercounted row 43 and overclaimed rows 40 and 42 at once. Declined: editing `security-checklist-v1.md` here to split the two rows — that is F21's edit, and this spec's accounting table is its input.

Splitting it makes propagation structural. Narrows Q8, so it goes to the user (Gate 1 question 3). Declined: an override with a recorded platform version plus an owner nag when it goes stale (more machinery, and it still fails silently if the nag is ignored).

Declined: a nested-merge argument on `merge_settings` (`settings || jsonb_build_object('privacy', COALESCE(settings->'privacy','{}'::jsonb) || :patch)`) — correct and still one statement, but it changes a shipped repository method for one caller where a required-field contract costs no SQL at all. **Recorded cost**: a future third field under `privacy` must be required too, or force the nested merge.

Keying on the id makes every outcome honest and changes the manage flow's shape not at all. Declined: a lookup-stable derived column (`phone_erased_from_hash`) — a second permanent derivation of the number the endpoint exists to destroy, to save the caller a field it already holds.
- **D18 — The erase transaction takes the per-tenant advisory lock as its first statement.** `create_booking` and `reschedule` both take it, and `upsert`'s docstring states it is lock-free *because every caller already holds it*. Without it, a concurrent booking's `existing.name = <her real name>` blocks on the erase's row lock and applies after it commits — leaving `erased_at` set, the phone scrubbed, and her real name restored, plus the live future booking the 409 guard exists to prevent. One line; makes the guard and the scrub sets atomic against the public path.
- **D19 — The audit trail records `customer_id` + `phone_last4` + a capped `reason`, never a full number or a name, and the export is audited too.** The first draft contradicted itself — "field names and counts only, never values" in one section, "audited with the actor and the phone" in another — over the one table exempt from every retention class **forever**. `owner.py:89-92` already settled this for the codebase: `_last4`, *"the most of a number that may be written down"*. `reason` is capped at 500 bytes and its field hint says record why, never who; a test asserts no subject identifier reaches `details`. The export gains its own action because checklist row 38 covers "data **access** by operators", not only mutations.
- **D20 — Marketing consent is written by its own guarded UPDATE, called on both the new-booking and the 0009-replay path.** The first draft named `upsert` as the writer and justified replay safety with a claim about `upsert`'s "only when NULL" rule — but `service.py:296` returns from the replay branch *before* `upsert` is reached at `:313`, so the writer was unreachable there and a ticked box on a replayed submission recorded nothing. A consent is an affirmative act independent of whether a booking is new, and a consent dropped by statement order is not a decision. Declined: documenting the drop instead of fixing it (it would be a decision made by accident, then ratified); and threading a flag through `upsert` (it keys on phone and would not be reached on the replay path anyway).
- **D21 — The retention runner enumerates every tenant row, not `list_active()`.** `list_active()` filters `deleted_at IS NULL AND status = 'active'`, and both `suspend()` and `soft_delete()` are shipped operator commands — so pinning enumeration to `poll_once`'s shape meant a boutique suspended for non-payment or churned kept every name, phone, note and SMS body forever, with no clock. The retention duty does not lapse when the controller's account does, and this spec's own rule that a boutique may not choose its own retention has to bind through suspension too. New `TenantsRepository.list_all()` for the runner; `list_active()` stays the poller's, where skipping a suspended tenant is correct. Terminal purge on off-boarding is out of scope (Risk 11).
- **D22 — A policy's predicate must be falsified by its own action, and the registry asserts it.** The first draft's `customers` SCRUB fed on "orphaned and older than 30 days" with no `erased_at IS NULL`, so it re-scrubbed its own output every tick: `erased_at` overwritten hourly, all 50 chunks burned on already-done rows, and — under a stable ordering — the not-yet-scrubbed rows behind them never reached. Both of its listed tests passed regardless. The predicate is fixed here; the invariant is asserted over `POLICIES` as a loop, because F38's staff SCRUB inherits this exact shape and should inherit the guard with it.
- **D23 — `retention`'s `--operator` is `required=True`, deliberately diverging from `_with_operator`.** `cli.py:43,47-50` default it to `$USER` on all five shipped subcommands, so the first draft's test assertion would simply have failed. The divergence is the right resolution, not the assertion: the other five are recoverable or read-only, this one issues an irreversible multi-tenant hard-DELETE, and `$USER` on a shared box is not an audit identity. Both behaviours are pinned by test so the difference reads as intent.
